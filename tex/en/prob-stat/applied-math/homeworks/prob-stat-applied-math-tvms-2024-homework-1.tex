\begin{problem}
This problem examines in more detail the notion of a sigma-algebra on a set.
Recall that a family $\mathcal F$ of subsets of a set $\Omega$ is a sigma-algebra
(respectively, an algebra) on $\Omega$ if:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\Omega\in\mathcal F$;
\item if $A\in\mathcal F$, then $A^c:=\Omega\setminus A\in\mathcal F$;
\item if $A_1,A_2,\ldots$ are countably many (respectively, finitely many for an
algebra) elements of $\mathcal F$, then $\bigcup_i A_i\in\mathcal F$.
\end{itemize}
For brevity, ``$\sigma$-algebra'' below means ``$\sigma$-algebra on $\Omega$''.
\begin{enumerate}
\item (2 points) Give an example of an algebra (on some $\Omega$ that you construct)
that is not a sigma-algebra.
\item (2 points) Is every sigma-algebra closed under intersections of countably many of its elements?
\item (2 points) Prove that an intersection of $\sigma$-algebras is a $\sigma$-algebra.
State whether you assume the intersection is finite, countable, or something else.
\end{enumerate}
Let $\mathcal G\subset 2^\Omega$ be a class of subsets of $\Omega$. Define the
sigma-algebra generated by $\mathcal G$, denoted $\sigma(\mathcal G)$, by
\[\sigma(\mathcal G):=\bigcap_{\substack{\mathcal A\text{ is a }\sigma\text{-algebra}\\\mathcal G\subset\mathcal A}}\mathcal A.\]
\begin{enumerate}[start=4]
\item (2 points) Prove that the definition is well defined, and that the above intersection is nonempty.
\end{enumerate}
If $\Omega$ is a topological space, so that, roughly speaking, we can define
$\mathcal O$ as the collection of open subsets of $\Omega$, define the Borel
$\sigma$-algebra on $\Omega$ by $\mathcal B(\Omega):=\sigma(\mathcal O)$.
\begin{enumerate}[start=5]
\item (2 points) Consider $\Omega=\mathbb R$. Is $\mathcal O$ countable or
uncountable? Can we construct a countable $\widetilde{\mathcal O}$ such that
$\mathcal B(\mathbb R)=\sigma(\widetilde{\mathcal O})$?
\item (2 points) Again let $\Omega=\mathbb R$. Prove that $\mathcal B(\mathbb R)$
is generated by sets of each of the following forms, for $a,b\in\mathbb R$:
\begin{enumeratea}
\item $(a,b)$;
\item $[a,b]$;
\item $(a,b]$;
\item $(a,\infty)$;
\item $[a,\infty)$.
\end{enumeratea}
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
\begin{problem}
(2 points) Which do you think is more likely: rolling at least $n$ sixes with
$6n$ dice, or at least $(n+1)$ sixes with $6(n+1)$ dice? Prove your answer.
\end{problem}
